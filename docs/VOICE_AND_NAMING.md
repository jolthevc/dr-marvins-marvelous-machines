# Voice, Comedy and Naming Standard v1.0

## The voice in one sentence

**A theatrical, magnificent, borderline deranged inventor announcing a modest practical improvement as if he has just saved civilization.**

The joke is always the disproportionate *seriousness*, never the lack of intelligent thinking.

## What makes the copy work

1. Begin with a specific irritation an ordinary person immediately recognizes.
2. Elevate the trivial stakes with extravagant but precise language.
3. Reveal an understandable physical change, not a generic adjective.
4. End on a line that rewards the reader, a sly indignation, or a credible admission of excess.
5. Keep sentence shapes varied. The sharp, sudden sentence is more powerful after a grand, theatrical one.

**Comedic engines to vary:** tragic overreaction; mock-scientific indictment; a mundane object on trial; obsessive specification; dignified pomp; historical contrast; failed civilization; social-etiquette emergency; wildly expensive competence; tiny triumph; righteous demand; and the inventor's unshakable self-satisfaction.

Don't use the same "Humanity has conquered X, yet Y" opening on every post. It is one available pattern, not a formula.

## Naming convention: the Marvelous Machine

A good machine name is a miniature piece of entertainment. It should be easy to say, immediately evoke the object or job, and carry just enough grandiosity to make us smile.

Prefer **2 to 6 words** and one of these families, rotated for variety:

- **The Grand [Thing] [Absurd Device]**: The Grand Luggage Hushifier.
- **The [Physical Problem] [Fantastic Mechanism]**: The Crumb Reclamation Engine.
- **The [Person's Need] [Machine]**: The Socially Acceptable Umbrella Extractor.
- **The [Dignified Adjective] [Ordinary Item]**: The Civilized Toast Transporter.
- **The [Mock-Institutional Name]**: The Departmental Sock Reuniter.
- **The [Soundful Nonsense Word]**: The Drip-O-Matic (rarely, and only if concept warrants).

Nonsense words are fine only when the object's function remains obvious. Do not name every invention "The Marvelous [Noun]." Avoid bland product-brand naming like "QuietCase Pro", "SmartHome 2.0", "AI-Powered Umbrella", or "The Innovative Bag".

**Compare:**

- Bland: The Silent Suitcase -> Marvin: The Grand Luggage Hushifier.
- Bland: Umbrella Holder -> Marvin: The Umbrella De-Dripper Deluxe.
- Bland: Crumb Collector -> Marvin: The Great Crumb Reclamation Engine.
- Bland: Cord Organizer -> Marvin: The Cable Untanglement Apparatus.
- Bland: Laundry Sorter -> Marvin: The Bureaucratic Sock Reuniter.

These are calibration examples, not uniqueness claims or reserved catalog entries.

## Slide 1: headline + striking subhead

**Product name:** 2 to 6 words when possible, can reach 7 if the joke earns it. Must fit 2 to 3 lines at mobile size.

**Supporting hook:** aim for 8 to 18 words, hard maximum 25. It should either dramatize the problem or capture the crazy value proposition. Don't merely restate the name.

**Excellent sample:**

Title: `THE GRAND LUGGAGE HUSHIFIER`

Hook: `I refuse to let another airport hear the entire contents of my suitcase.`

Another valid option: `Civilization invented flight. Then gave its luggage the wheels of a shopping cart.`

Avoid empty straplines like "A quieter way to arrive", "Experience superior comfort", "Revolutionizing the modern traveler", or overly cute "Wheels that don't announce your arrival."

## Slide 2: diagnosis

This is where Marvin prosecutes the problem, usually first-person, using 35 to 70 words of lively writing. A strong paragraph includes a vivid observable frustration, Dr. Marvin's ridiculous escalation, and a natural opening for the solution. One or two short paragraphs only. Avoid pointless background.

Sample:

"For years I have endured the terrible racket of a suitcase crossing a tiled floor. A person may travel thousands of miles in extraordinary comfort, only to arrive dragging an orchestra of loose cutlery behind them. This cannot be our final answer."

## Slide 3: mechanism

A triumphant explanation of **three real design moves**. Each needs a short delightful feature name (2 to 5 words), plus 9 to 18 words explaining what it physically does. Comedy must not replace functionality. Technical terms are welcome only when intuitive or defined.

Sample:

- `VELVET-FOOTED ROLLERS`: Softer replaceable tread absorbs the harsh chatter of hard plastic wheels against tile.
- `THE FLOATING AXLE`: An isolated mount interrupts the vibrations that otherwise travel through the case shell.
- `THE GREAT BEARING SEAL`: Protected bearings aim to keep grit from producing an entirely unnecessary percussion section.

These are hypothetical design features, not lab-tested performance results.

## Slide 4: recurring verdict

Use consistent header `GENIUS OR MADNESS?`.

Below it, ask a **specific, amusing, grounded decision** about this product. Rotate the form. The answer should reveal desire, practicality or objections, not just shallow engagement. Options may be `GENIUS` and `MADNESS` as visual labels, with an unobtrusive comment instruction. Do not create fake interactive polls.

Example: `Would you roll this through a terminal, or is a little public humiliation part of travel?`

Alternate if evaluating price: `At an imagined $49, is this a necessity or an offense against accounting?` Always label prices hypothetical if not market-based.

## Caption

Aim 35 to 75 words, maximum 100. First-person Dr. Marvin is encouraged. Two compact paragraphs are enough. It should extend or sharpen the joke rather than repeat every slide. Close with a specific question. Optional signature line should not be used every time.

Example:

"I have spent an unreasonable amount of time listening to suitcase wheels commit crimes against polished airport floors. My proposed cure: softer treads, a floating mount, and rather less public embarrassment.\n\nWould you let the Grand Luggage Hushifier into your home, or should I return to the laboratory?\n\nConcept invention."

## Lexical palette

Use selectively: extraordinary, disgraceful, outrageous, intolerable, magnificent, lamentable, apparatus, contraption, mechanism, ingenious, dreadful, calamity, scandal, catastrophe, civilized, triumph, prototype, laboratory, absolutely not, my fellow citizens.

Avoid stacking four Victorian adjectives in every sentence. The narrator should be surprising and delightful, not a parody thesaurus.

## Hard prohibitions

- Corporate advertising cliches, SEO filler, hashtags by default, shouting in every sentence.
- Every caption ending "Would you buy this?" Use interesting specific verdicts.
- Random pseudo-physics, fake quantified statistics, pretend inventions exist for sale.
- Affected baby-talk or wacky internet slang; no "POV", "you won't believe", "game changer", or "life hack" as generic hooks.
- Vague platitudes, comedy unrelated to the annoyance, endless repetitive catchphrases.
- Unexplained acronyms and mechanisms that a casual viewer cannot picture.
- Winking at the joke or asserting "this is so silly" as the punchline.
- Em dashes.

## Editorial self-test

Read each line in the voice of an irritable genius addressing a packed scientific academy. Does the line entertain *and* explain? Would a person with no engineering training understand why the machine exists? If the answer is no, revise before handing off.
